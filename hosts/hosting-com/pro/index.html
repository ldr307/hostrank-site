What hosting.com Pro includes

The official data lists three concrete things for this plan: 50 GB web space, up to 10 websites and 20 staging websites. For a single WordPress blog, 50 GB web space is the number to keep in mind, because uploaded images, theme files, plugins and the database all count against it. A text-heavy blog with moderately sized images tends to sit well inside that figure for a long time, while a photography blog that uploads full-resolution files will reach it sooner.

The allowance of up to 10 websites means the plan is not limited to one blog. A blogger who later wants a separate portfolio site, a newsletter landing page or a second blog on another topic can add them under the same account without buying another plan. The 20 staging websites are copies of a site used for testing. In practice, that lets a blogger try a new theme, a major plugin update or a WordPress core update on a staging copy first, and only touch the live blog once the change works.

How billing and the term work

Billing for hosting.com Pro is listed as USD per month on a 4-year term, paid upfront. The term is 48 months. In plain terms, the monthly figure in the price table is a way of expressing the cost, but the whole 48 months is charged at once when the order is placed. The total due today is also in the table, so compare both numbers before checkout rather than reading only the per-month line.

This matters for a blogger because the decision is a long one. Paying upfront for 48 months means the money is committed even if the blog changes direction, moves to another host or goes quiet after a year. The official data does not list a renewal price for this plan, so what happens at the end of the 48 months in terms of cost is unknown from the data; confirm the renewal price with hosting.com before paying. The data also does not describe any refund or cancellation window, so that is a second thing to confirm directly with the provider.

Who this plan suits

hosting.com Pro suits a blogger who has already decided to stick with a blog for several years and prefers to settle hosting once rather than think about it again. It also suits someone who expects to run more than one site, since up to 10 websites are covered, or who wants a safe place to test changes, since 20 staging websites are included. Shared Hosting of this kind means the provider manages the server itself, so there is no operating system to patch, no web server to configure and no command line to maintain; the blogger works through whatever control panel the host provides.

It fits less well for someone starting a first blog who is not yet sure the project will last. The upfront commitment is the main reason: the whole 48 months is paid at once. A blogger in that position may prefer a plan with a shorter term, even at a different monthly figure, and revisit the choice once the blog has found its footing. It also fits less well for anyone who needs the resource details that the official data does not list, which the next section covers.

What the official data does not confirm

A WordPress blogger usually wants to know a few things beyond web space and site counts, and the official data for hosting.com Pro is silent on all of them. It does not mention a WordPress installer or any WordPress-specific tooling, an SSL certificate, backups or restores, email accounts, a bandwidth or traffic allowance, CPU or RAM, support hours or channels, server locations, or any uptime commitment. None of this means those things are absent; it means they cannot be confirmed from the pricing data used for this page.

Before signing up, check each of these on the provider's own site or by asking its sales or support team. For a blog, the order of importance is usually: whether WordPress can be installed from the control panel, whether an SSL certificate is included, how backups work and how far back they go, and whether email on the blog's domain is part of the plan or sold separately. Each of these affects both the setup steps later on this page and the ongoing cost of running the blog.

What to check before signing up

Start with the price table on this page and confirm that the total charged today, not just the per-month figure, fits the budget. Then confirm the renewal price and the cancellation or refund terms with hosting.com, since neither appears in the official data. Next, run through the unconfirmed items listed above, especially WordPress installation, an SSL certificate, backups and email, and get a clear answer on each before paying.

Finally, think about the blog's likely size over the next four years. If it will stay a single site with modest images, the 50 GB web space and 20 staging websites may be more than needed, and hosting.com Starter or a 12 months Namecheap plan could be the more proportionate choice. If it will grow into several sites or a media-heavy publication, the Pro limits are the ones that leave room.

Practical setup guide

  1. STEP 1Set up the blog and a staging copy

    After signing up, log in to the hosting.com control panel and install WordPress on the account. The plan data does not mention how WordPress is installed, so look for an installer in the panel or follow the provider's own guide. Once the live site exists, create one of the 20 staging websites as a copy so that theme and plugin experiments happen there rather than on the blog readers will see.

  2. STEP 2Point the domain at the new hosting

    In the account where the domain is registered, change its nameservers to the ones hosting.com provides, or add the DNS records the host specifies. If the domain already serves a live blog elsewhere, finish building the new site first and only switch DNS once it is ready. To confirm which nameservers the domain currently uses, run this on a local machine and replace the placeholder with the real domain: dig +short NS example.com

  3. STEP 3Enable HTTPS once the domain resolves

    An SSL certificate is one of the items the plan data does not confirm, so check with hosting.com whether one is included and how it is enabled. Wait until the domain points at the new hosting, then turn on the certificate through the control panel and set WordPress to use the https address in its general settings. To confirm the site answers over HTTPS and redirects correctly, run this locally and replace the placeholder with the real domain: curl -I https://example.com

  4. STEP 4Find out how backups and restores work

    Backups are also not confirmed in the plan data, so ask hosting.com whether automatic backups are included, how often they run and how a restore is triggered. Regardless of the answer, keep an independent copy of the blog by using a WordPress backup plugin or by downloading files and the database from the control panel. Test a restore on a staging website once so that the process is familiar before it is ever needed.

  5. STEP 5Record the billing details

    Write down the date the plan was ordered and the date the 48 months term ends, since the whole term was paid upfront. The renewal price is not listed in the official data, so contact hosting.com to confirm it and note the answer alongside the renewal date. Also record the provider's cancellation and refund terms, which are not in the data either, so there are no surprises when the term runs out.
